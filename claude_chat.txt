● Here's the clear version.

  What positional encoding would have fixed

  NRMS's UserEncoder is permutation-invariant: it self-attends over your 20
  clicked-article vectors, then pools them with additive attention. Both
  operations are order-blind. So the model genuinely cannot tell your most
  recent click from your oldest — shuffle your history and the user vector is
  identical. Positional encoding fixes exactly that, by adding a learned "this
  is slot i" vector to each position.

  That's a real defect, and it was a reasonable proposal.

  Why we didn't do it

  1. The padding measurement, which is decisive.

  adapter.py:29-33 left-pads the history, and layers.py:11 applies no attention
  masking — pad slots participate in attention, matching the reference
  implementation. Measured at HISTORY_SIZE = 20 on the test split:

  ┌─────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────┬────────────┐
  │                             │         MIND         │  EB-NeRD   │
  ├─────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────┼────────────┤
  │ impressions with <20 clicks │ 51.1%                │ 4.3%       │
  ├─────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────┼────────────┤
  │ mean PAD slots (of 20)      │ 5.9 → 29.4% of input │ 0.4 → 1.8% │
  ├─────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────┼────────────┤
  │ median history length       │ 19                   │ 226        │
  └─────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────┴────────────┘

  On MIND, over half of users don't fill the history buffer, and nearly a third
  of the slots are padding. Because padding is on the left, those pads occupy
  positions 0…k−1 — so adding positional encoding means attaching position
  embeddings to padding, on the dataset where padding dominates.

  It's worse than wasted: since the pad count equals 20 − len(history), position
  embeddings on pad slots encode history length, confounded with the position
  signal you actually wanted. PE and masking are complementary, and PE without
  masking is compromised precisely where it would matter most.

  2. The category argument — this is the real reason.

  Both candidates fix something, but not the same kind of something:

  - Order is information NRMS has but can't access, because the encoder is
    permutation-invariant. The model has partial routes around it — the pad
    vector is constant, so additive attention can learn to down-weight it.
  - Article age is not in NRMS's input at all. It cannot be derived from title
    tokens at any amount of training or any architecture tweak. The channel is
    simply absent.

  Adding a channel the model structurally lacks is a surer bet than improving
  access to one it already has and can partly compensate for. That's a
  difference in kind, not magnitude — which is what you want when you get one
  arm.

  3. Independent evidence that freshness was the right channel.

  - Your Q2 re-ranker put 80–87% of its gain on freshness_log_hours for EB-NeRD.
  - Median clicked-article age at click time is 3.1 hours.
  - "Freshness weighting" is on the A2 doc's own list of suggested improvements.

  So the signal was already demonstrated to dominate in a different model on the
  same data, before you spent a single epoch on it.

  Ranked for the record: freshness > attention masking > positional encoding.
  Masking came second because it costs zero new parameters and fixes the root
  problem (padding in attention) that cripples PE — if you had a second arm,
  masking is the one to run, not PE.

  One honesty note for the design note: PE was rejected on the padding
  measurement, not on an A/B test of PE itself. That's a legitimate basis — the
  measurement predicts the failure mode directly — but word it as "rejected on
  measured padding occupancy," not "we tested it and it lost." Same discipline
  as the rank_xendcg issue.

  explanations.

  \textbf{It is not a coverage gap.} 99.9\% of MIND test candidates and 99.9\%
  of

  Recall is a hard ceiling on stage 2 — the re-ranker cannot recover a click
  that was never retrieved — so popular wins on the only thing that matters
  first.

  Why four and not two: the arms are constructed so consecutive pairs isolate
  one change each.

  - bm25 → bm25_fresh changes the search space (whole catalogue → 1 h display
    pool) while holding the scoring function fixed. EB-NeRD 2.55% → 84.2%.
  - bm25_fresh → popular changes the ranking signal (history-title BM25 → recent
    popularity) while holding the pool fixed. EB-NeRD 84.2% → 97.0%, MIND 21.0%
    → 93.9%.

  That's what lets you say the A1 failure had two independent causes rather than
  one. semantic is the control on the first arm: it shows the catalogue-wide
  failure isn't specific to lexical matching, since the embedding retriever
  fails the same way (2.49%/3.87%).

  Q1. Wire under load [30 marks]

  The system. Wire is the recommendation back-end of a news site. At the busy
  hour N = 1000 readers are in a session. A reader requests a feed, waits for it
  (response time R), reads for Z = 4.8 s, then requests the next feed. Sessions
  are long, so treat the reader population as fixed. In steady state Wire
  serves X = 200 feed requests/s.

  (a) [12] A breaking-news event is running when the network partitions between
  the region holding the click-event stream and the region serving feeds, for
  about 20 minutes. Four subsystems are affected: the fresh-pool display log
  (which articles were shown in the last hour), the as-of article counters
  (click/display counts and CTR), the user click history, and the impression log
  that becomes tomorrow's training data. Counters are already consumed with a
  10-minute reporting lag. The impression log is the sole source of training
  labels and of every feature's as-of ordering.

  For each subsystem state the choice on the partitioned P branch (A or C), and
  in one line name the invariant protected or sacrificed.

  (b) [8] Using Little's law at two boundaries — one including the reading
  users, one excluding them — derive R in terms of N, X, Z and compute it. Then,
  given a measured single-core service time of 5.88 ms/request, state how many
  cores are needed to sustain X = 200/s, and how much of the R budget the server
  actually consumes. State the assumption the derivation needs.

  (c) [10] The click/impression stream runs at 5 × 10⁴ append-only events/s. The
  as-of counter store holds one 8-byte timestamp per event.
  1. If counters are kept in a B+-tree with 4 KB pages updated in place, compute
     the write amplification relative to the 8 bytes that logically change, and
     the device write rate in MB/s. Compare against an LSM-tree that appends
     ~32 bytes per event and compacts with a further ≈10× amplification.
  2. Which engine for the event log, and which structure for the query-time
     as-of lookup? Justify from the access pattern.

  Answer key

  Q1(a) [12]

  Subsystem: Fresh-pool display log
  P: A
  Invariant: Sacrifices candidate freshness. A stale pool degrades ranking
  quality but still returns a feed; no correctness invariant binds a
  recommendation.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Subsystem: As-of article counters
  P: A
  Invariant: Protects availability at zero extra cost — the consumer already
  tolerates a 10-minute click-reporting lag, so partition staleness is inside
  the tolerance the design already assumes.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Subsystem: User click history
  P: A
  Invariant: Sacrifices personalisation recency. Serving from last-known history

  loses a few clicks' worth of signal; nothing requires a feed to reflect every
   click.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Subsystem: Impression log
  P: C
  Invariant: The only genuine C. It is the sole source of training labels and of

  the as-of event ordering. Accepting writes whose timestamps/order cannot be
  reconstructed silently poisons every downstream feature — a corruption that
  surfaces months later as leakage, not as an error. Buffer and backfill; never
   admit unordered events.

  The point of the question: in a recommender almost everything is A, because
  recommendations have no correctness invariant — only quality. The one C is the
  offline training path, and it's C for a reason that has nothing to do with
  user-visible correctness.

  Q1(b) [8]

  Outer boundary (includes reading users): all N readers are either waiting or
  reading, so
   N = X(R + Z).
  Inner boundary (server only): in-flight requests = X·R.

  Rearranging: R = N/X − Z = 1000/200 − 4.8 = 5 − 4.8 = 0.2 s = 200 ms.

  Cores: single-core service time 5.88 ms → 170 req/s per core. 200/170 = 1.18 →
  2 cores (1.18 before any headroom for peak).

  Budget: the server consumes 5.88 ms of a 200 ms budget — ~3%; the other 97% is
  network, client render and slack. p99 of 10.5 ms is ~19× inside a 100 ms SLA.

  Assumption: flow balance — arrival rate equals completion rate over the
  interval, with a fixed population and no abandonment.

  Q1(c) [10]

  1. B+-tree writes a full 4 KB page per 8-byte change:
   amplification = 4096/8 = 512×; rate = 5×10⁴ × 4096 = 204.8 MB/s.
  LSM appends 32 B and compacts ≈10×: 320 B/event → 5×10⁴ × 320 = 16 MB/s, i.e.
  12.8× less.

  2. Event log → LSM. The workload is pure append at high rate with no in-place
  updates; B+-tree's page-granularity in-place writes are the worst possible
  match, and an LSM's read penalty doesn't matter because nothing does point
  lookups on raw events.

  Query-time as-of lookup → neither; an in-memory sorted array. The query is not
  "fetch a record" but "how many events for article a occurred strictly before
  t" — a range question answered by binary search. That's exactly
  TrainEventIndex: per-article sorted int64 arrays in array.array("q"), queried
  with np.searchsorted. 8 bytes/event, 68.1 MiB for MIND's 8.93M events, O(log
  n) per lookup, no engine required. The right answer is that the storage-engine
  question is the wrong question for the read path.

  Dataset asymmetry: MIND has no session_id (sessionised by a 30-min idle gap
  instead), no dwell time (feature is all-NaN with an availability flag), and no
  published_time (0 of 65,238 articles) — so freshness uses an
  earliest-sighting proxy. EB-NeRD has all three. The two datasets therefore
  form a natural ablation of exactly those signals.

  Arithmetic: 54,222 training impressions × (1 positive + 49 random negatives) ≈
  2,732,651 rows × 24 features × 8 B = 525 MB dense float64. Kept in RAM by:
  capping MIND training at 60k impressions, sampling 49 random negatives rather
  than keeping all 200, flushing accumulator dicts every 250k rows (dicts cost
  ~1.8 KB/row vs ~195 B/row in the frame), and evaluating in 5,000-impression
  chunks so no pass holds more than one chunk.

  Boundary: article_stats.TrainEventIndex stores, per article, a sorted int64-ns
  array of event times; as_of(article_id, t) is np.searchsorted(times, t,
  side="left") — strictly before t, so a row's own outcome can never enter its
  own feature. Clock allowance: CLICK_REPORTING_LAG_MINUTES = 10 — clicks are
  only counted from impressions before t − 10 min, because a click lands after
  the impression that produced it. The leakage tests in
  tests/test_feature_engineering.py fail if this breaks; the whole suite is 148
  tests.

  Q2(b) [25] — MIND

  Stage 1: popular — the articles the platform displayed in the previous hour,
  ranked by 10-min-lagged click counts, with a 24 h backfill when the 1 h pool
  holds < K. K = 200. Recall@50/100/200 = 81.6 / 88.6 / 93.9%.

  The number that decided it: A1's catalogue-wide retriever recovers the click
  for only 2.90% of impressions at K=200 — because clicks go to what is in
  circulation (EB-NeRD's median clicked article is 3.1 h old), while the
  catalogue reaches back to 2000. Restricting the search space to the 1 h pool
  and changing the ranking signal to popularity are two separate fixes: 2.90% →
  21.0% → 93.9%.

  Stage 2: LambdaMART (LightGBM objective="lambdarank") — RankNet pairwise
  gradients weighted by |ΔnDCG|. Non-default: lambdarank_truncation_level = 200
  (full group) so the ΔnDCG weights reflect the entire 200-item list the metrics
  are computed over. 119 trees. Top gain: position_bias 56.6%, retrieval_rank
  11.2%, history_embedding_cosine 10.6%.

  Serving: stage 1 0.54 ms / features 2.72 ms / scoring 0.14 ms → p50 3.47 ms,
  p99 8.00 ms. Memory: embedding matrix 95.6, BM25 index 36.0, as-of counters
  68.1, fresh-pool log 26.5, booster 3.9 MiB ≈ 292 MiB. 253 QPS single-core,
  $0.000186/1k at $0.17/vCPU-h, p99 12× inside the 100 ms SLA.

  Consistency check: the harness is strictly serial (N = 1, one request at a
  time), so X = 1/R̄ holds exactly, not approximately: 1/3.949 ms = 253.2 /s,
  which is the reported QPS to one decimal. EB-NeRD likewise: 1/5.883 ms =
  170.0. Mechanisms that bought this: precomputed article embeddings (one dot
  product per candidate, no encoding at request time), one BM25 query and one
  pooled user vector per impression rather than per candidate, and a per-process
  cached scorer so chunked evaluation builds the BM25 index once.

  Q2(c) [20]

  Metrics: AUC, MRR, nDCG@5, nDCG@10, plus diversity, novelty, coverage.
  Accuracy metrics are averaged over the full impression population —
  impressions whose click stage 1 never retrieved score 0 rather than being
  dropped, because dropping them would silently redefine the population between
  arms. Labels are the label == 1 entries of each impression's own in-view list.

  Baseline + improvement: NRMS (Wu 2019) reproduced against hparams_nrms;
  improvement is a freshness term added to the logit, score = ⟨u,c⟩ +
  g(log1p(age), known), g a 2→16→1 MLP (65 params, zero-init so both arms start
  bit-identical). EB-NeRD: AUC +0.0686 [+0.0662, +0.0710], CI nowhere near zero.

  Where it fails: MIND — every delta < 0.0015. The explanation is median
  within-impression age spread: 27.5 h (MIND) vs 1,057 h (EB-NeRD). An additive
  term nearly constant across a row cannot reorder that row. Ruled out: coverage
  (99.9% of MIND candidates carry an age) and the proxy (EB-NeRD has both age
  definitions; swapping real publish times for first-seen costs only 0.013 AUC).
  Entitled to claim: "freshness-as-first-seen does not help on MIND." Not
  entitled to: "freshness does not help on MIND" — the proxy is right-censored.

  Slices and ablations. Stage-1 arms (bm25 → bm25_fresh → popular) separate
  wrong-search-space from wrong-ranking-signal. Q9 drops serving-unavailable
  features. no_history_content prices the two Q1.1 similarity features: on MIND
  the re-ranker beats stage 1 by +0.0164 [+0.0148, +0.0181] with them and
  +0.0003 [−0.0012, +0.0017] without — the entire gain.

  The weak slice: on EB-NeRD, impressions whose clicked article is in the top
  20% by train click count score nDCG@5 of exactly 0.0000 (MRR 0.0072) — the
  click is never placed in the top 5. This follows directly from the design: a
  head article is by construction an old article, and a re-ranker putting 52.9%
  of its gain on freshness_log_hours buries exactly those. The system is tuned
  to the news cycle and pays for it on enduring content. Only 197 impressions
  (0.8%), so the headline barely moves — but it is the clearest statement of
  what the design trades away.

  A two-stage retrieve-then-rank news recommender, evaluated on EB-NeRD and

  \section{Data Pipeline}
  Both datasets are parsed into one schema: an articles table (title, abstract,
  body, category, entities, published\_time) and a behaviors table (one row
  per impression: history, candidates, labels, timestamp). EB-NeRD additionally
  carries session\_id and per-history-click dwell times; MIND has neither, and
  that asymmetry recurs throughout the results.

  Splits are temporal rather than random, to prevent future leakage during
  training. Article features such as freshness, popularity and CTR are computed
  strictly from data before the impression time $t$, and within-session
  features count only impressions that occurred earlier in the same session.

  \section{Design Choices}
      \subsection{Re-Ranker Objective}
      The choices were pointwise \textit{binary} , LambdaMART and the list wise
  \textit{rank\_xendcg}. RankNet was rejected because it minimizes the number
  of inversions and doesnt treat the inversion between rank 1, 2 and rank 100,
  101 differently. The pointwise is rejected because they predict the absolute
  relevance score of each item in isolation, completely ignoring how those
  items compare to each other.

      \subsection{NRMS improvement (Q3)}
      The initial idea was adding the positional encoding on the user encoder.
  It was rejected on measurement: at \texttt{HISTORY\_SIZE}~=~20,
  \textbf{51.1\%} of MIND impressions carry fewer than 20 clicks and
  \textbf{29.4\%} of history slots are padding, and \texttt{layers.py} applies
  no masking, so PE would attach position embeddings to padding on exactly the
  dataset where padding dominates. \textbf{Freshness} won on a category
  difference rather than a magnitude one: article age is not in NRMS's input
  \emph{at all} and cannot be learned from title tokens at any amount of
  training.

  1. What stage 1 is for

  A user opens the news site. We have to pick what to show. Scoring all 11,777
  EB-NeRD articles carefully for every user is too slow, so the work is split in
  two:

  - Stage 1 (candidate generation): quickly pick about 200 articles the user
    might click.
  - Stage 2 (re-ranker): order those 200 carefully with LambdaMART.

  Stage 1 sets a hard ceiling. If the article the user actually clicked isn't
  among the 200, stage 2 can never put it at the top. So the one number that
  matters for stage 1 is recall@200: how often the clicked article is somewhere
  in the 200.

  2. Why the A1 approach failed

  A1 compared your click history against the entire catalogue and picked the 200
  most similar articles. That recovered the click only 2.55% of the time.

  The reason is how news works. People click whatever is on screen right now.
  The median clicked article is 3.1 hours old, but the catalogue goes back to
  2000. A1 was searching 20 years of articles for something published this
  morning.

  So popular stops asking "what matches this user's past?" and asks "what was
  the site showing in the last hour, and which of those were people clicking?"

  3. What popular does, in one sentence

  ▎ For an impression at time t, take every article the site displayed in the
  ▎ last hour, rank them by how many clicks they got (ignoring the most recent
  ▎ 10 minutes), and return the top 200.

  4. The three windows

  At time t, FreshPool keeps three running counts:

  ┌──────────┬─────────────┬────────────────────┬───────────────────────────┐
  │  Window  │ Time range  │   What it counts   │            Why            │
  ├──────────┼─────────────┼────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┤
  │ displays │ [t − 1h, t) │ how often each     │ defines the pool: what's  │
  │          │             │ article was shown  │ in circulation            │
  ├──────────┼─────────────┼────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┤
  │          │ [t − 1h, t  │ how often each     │                           │
  │ clicks   │ − 10min)    │ article was        │ the ranking signal        │
  │          │             │ clicked            │                           │
  ├──────────┼─────────────┼────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┤
  │ backfill │ [t − 24h,   │ displays over a    │ a spare supply if the 1 h │
  │          │ t)          │ day                │  pool has fewer than 200  │
  └──────────┴─────────────┴────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┘

  In code, fresh_pool.py:119-121:

  self._displays.advance(t - self._window,       t)                # [t-1h,  t)
  self._backfill.advance(t - self._backfill_span, t)                # [t-24h, t)
  self._clicks.advance(  t - self._window,       t - self._lag)     # [t-1h,
  t-10min)

  Why the clicks window stops 10 minutes early: a click happens after the
  impression that produced it. If someone was shown an article 2 minutes ago,
  they may not have clicked yet. Counting those clicks would mean using
  information from the future, which is leakage (Q9). The 10-minute lag avoids
  it.

  6. The code, step by step

  Step 1 — load_log: build the timeline (fresh_pool.py:157)

  for s in ("train", "val", "test"):
      frame = pd.read_parquet(processed_dir / f"behaviors_{s}.parquet", ...)
      frame["is_target"] = s == split

  It reads all three splits, not only test. The display log is continuous in
  time: the first test impression needs to know what was shown in the last hour
  of val, so the windows need those rows. Only rows marked is_target get
  candidates generated for them; the rest just feed the counts. The log is
  trimmed to start 24 h before the first target impression and sorted by time.
  For EB-NeRD test that's 28,762 rows feeding 25,356 target impressions.

  Step 2 — _Window.advance: the two-pointer trick (fresh_pool.py:64-75)

  This is the efficient core. Each window keeps two pointers into the sorted
  log:

  while self._add < n and self._t[self._add] < hi:      # rows that just ENTERED
  the window
      for a in self._articles(self._add):
          self.counts[a] += 1
      self._add += 1
  while self._rm < self._add and self._t[self._rm] < lo:  # rows that just LEFT
  the window
      for a in self._articles(self._rm):
          self.counts[a] -= 1
          if self.counts[a] == 0: del self.counts[a]
      self._rm += 1

  Picture a window sliding right along a timeline. _add is its right edge and
  _rm is its left edge. When time moves forward, new rows are added on the right
  and old rows are subtracted on the left.

  Both pointers only ever move forward, so over the whole test set each row is
  added once and removed once. That's O(total events) instead of re-scanning an
  hour of data for every impression. The price is a strict rule, enforced at
  line 117: impressions must be processed in time order, or it raises
  ValueError.

  _articles() is the only difference between the windows: the clicks window
  counts only articles with label == 1, and the display windows count every
  article shown.

  Step 3 — popular(k): rank the pool (fresh_pool.py:137-142)

  top = heapq.nlargest(k, pool, key=lambda a: (clicks.get(a, 0), pool[a]))
  return self.backfill([(a, float(clicks.get(a, 0))) for a in top], k)

  - It ranks only articles in the pool (displayed in the last hour).
  - The sort key is the tuple (lagged clicks, displays). Python compares tuples
    element by element, so clicks come first and displays only break ties.
  - The score attached to each article is its click count. That's why
    retrieval_score for this arm is an integer.

  Step 4 — backfill: pad if short (fresh_pool.py:144-154)

  If the 1 h pool has fewer than 200 articles, the rest are filled from the 24 h
  window, most-displayed first, with score 0.0 so they rank below every real
  hit. If even 24 h is short (the first minutes of the dataset), the list stays
  short rather than inventing candidates.

  Step 5 — the driver loop (candidates.py:153-165)

  log  = fresh_pool.load_log(processed_dir, split)
  pool = fresh_pool.FreshPool(log)
  for row in log[log["is_target"]].itertuples(index=False):   # in time order
      pool.advance_to(row.t)                                   # slide windows
  to this moment
      ranked, used_fallback = pool.popular(k)                  # take top-200

  Then _to_wide saves everything to candidates_popular_test.parquet.

  9. Why there's no leakage

  Every count comes from events strictly before t:

  - Displays use < t (line 66: self._t[self._add] < hi with hi = t), so the
    impression never sees its own in-view list, or any impression sharing its
    exact timestamp.
  - Clicks stop at t − 10 min, so clicks that haven't happened yet are never
    counted.
  - Everything here is something a live site actually has: its own display log
    and its own click counters.

  10. What stage 2 receives

  For each impression, 200 rows of (impression_id, article_id, rank,
  retrieval_score), where retrieval_score is the lagged click count. The
  re-ranker turns these into features: retrieval_rank, position_bias =
  1/log(rank+2), and retrieval_score. It then adds everything popular ignored:
  freshness, CTR, category match, and the user's history similarity. Stage 1
  finds what's circulating; stage 2 decides what this user wants from it.

  1. The problem: why not just classify?

  The obvious approach is to train a classifier: "given these 24 features, is
  this article clicked? yes/no." That optimises accuracy of the probability.

  But you don't care about probabilities. You care about order. Two models can
  have identical accuracy while one puts the clicked article at rank 1 and the
  other at rank 50. And the metrics you report — MRR, nDCG@5 — care enormously
  about the top of the list and almost nothing about the bottom.

  LambdaMART exists to optimise order directly, and specifically to spend effort
  at the top.

  2. The name is a family tree, not three options

  This trips people up, so get it straight:

  ┌────────────┬──────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │            │ Year │                    What it added                     │
  ├────────────┼──────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │            │      │ Learn from pairs: "the clicked one should score      │
  │ RankNet    │ 2005 │ higher than this unclicked one." Loss =              │
  │            │      │ cross-entropy on that pairwise probability.          │
  ├────────────┼──────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │            │      │ Not a new loss — a modified gradient. Take RankNet's │
  │ LambdaRank │ 2006 │  gradient and multiply it by |ΔnDCG|, how much nDCG  │
  │            │      │ would change if you swapped those two items.         │
  ├────────────┼──────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ LambdaMART │ 2010 │ LambdaRank's gradients plugged into MART = Multiple  │
  │            │      │ Additive Regression Trees = gradient-boosted trees.  │
  └────────────┴──────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  So for a GBDT there aren't three pairwise choices. Choosing "pairwise for a
  GBDT" means LambdaMART. objective="lambdarank" in LightGBM is LambdaMART.

  3. Worked example — the numbers below are computed with your own
  src/metrics.py

  99.7% of your EB-NeRD impressions.

  nDCG@5 = 0.4307        ← the positive C is stuck at rank 4

  A one-line reminder of nDCG: with one relevant item, nDCG = 1/log₂(rank+1).
  Rank 1 → 1.0, rank 2 → 0.63, rank 3 → 0.5, rank 4 → 0.43, rank 5 → 0.39.
  Notice how steeply it drops early and how flat it gets later. That shape is
  the whole story.

  Now form every pair involving C and ask two questions.

  Question 1 — "how wrong is this pair right now?" (the RankNet part)
  1 / (1 + exp(s_C − s_j)). If C already beats j comfortably, this is near 0
  (nothing to fix). If C is far behind, near 1.

  Question 2 — "how much would nDCG improve if I fixed it?" (the Lambda part)
  Swap the two and recompute nDCG.

  Multiply them:

  ┌────────┬─────────────────┬─────────┬─────────┬─────────────┐
  │  pair  │ nDCG after swap │ |ΔnDCG| │ RankNet │ λ = product │
  ├────────┼─────────────────┼─────────┼─────────┼─────────────┤
  │ C vs A │ 1.0000          │ 0.5693  │ 0.8176  │ 0.4655      │
  ├────────┼─────────────────┼─────────┼─────────┼─────────────┤
  │ C vs B │ 0.6309          │ 0.2003  │ 0.7311  │ 0.1464      │
  ├────────┼─────────────────┼─────────┼─────────┼─────────────┤
  │ C vs D │ 0.5000          │ 0.0693  │ 0.6225  │ 0.0432      │
  ├────────┼─────────────────┼─────────┼─────────┼─────────────┤
  │ C vs E │ 0.3869          │ 0.0438  │ 0.3775  │ 0.0165      │
  └────────┴─────────────────┴─────────┴─────────┴─────────────┘

  Total upward push on C = 0.6716.

  The punchline

  Look at how that push is distributed, against what plain RankNet would do:

  ┌────────┬────────────────────┬─────────────────┐
  │  pair  │ LambdaMART's share │ RankNet's share │
  ├────────┼────────────────────┼─────────────────┤
  │ C vs A │ 69.3%              │ 32.1%           │
  ├────────┼────────────────────┼─────────────────┤
  │ C vs B │ 21.8%              │ 28.7%           │
  ├────────┼────────────────────┼─────────────────┤
  │ C vs D │ 6.4%               │ 24.4%           │
  ├────────┼────────────────────┼─────────────────┤
  │ C vs E │ 2.5%               │ 14.8%           │
  └────────┴────────────────────┴─────────────────┘

  RankNet spreads its effort almost evenly across all four pairs — beating E
  (rank 5) gets 15% of the effort, nearly half as much as beating A (rank 1).
  It's counting inversions, and an inversion is an inversion.

  LambdaMART puts 69% of its effort on the one pair that matters — overtaking A,
  because that's the swap that takes nDCG from 0.43 to 1.00. Beating E is worth
  almost nothing (0.4307 → 0.3869 is barely a change), so it gets 2.5%.

  That is the entire idea. The gradient is weighted by how much the metric would
  move. Since MRR and nDCG@5 are dominated by the top few positions, so is the
  training.

  Each negative gets the mirror-image push downward (−λ), so the total is
  conserved.

  4. Now the MART half: gradients → trees

  You have a λ for every row. What do you do with it?

  Each boosting round:

  1. Compute λᵢ for all ~1.09M training rows (EB-NeRD).
  2. Fit a regression tree that predicts λ from the 24 features. This is the key
     move — the tree learns which feature patterns identify rows that need
     pushing up.
  3. Add that tree's output × learning_rate (0.05) to every row's score.
  4. Repeat. Scores change → ranks change → new λ's → next tree.

  Your EB-NeRD model is 151 such trees, 63 leaves each. Here is its genuinely
  first tree, with thresholds decoded into human units:

  if position_bias <= 0.2684              (gain 74,987)   ← i.e. stage-1 rank
  worse than ~40
    then: if freshness_log_hours <= 2.047 (gain  6,861)   ← i.e. younger than
  ~6.7 hours
            then: if freshness_log_hours <= 1  ...        ← younger than ~1.7
  hours
            else: if position_bias <= 0.2358  ...         ← rank worse than ~50
    else: if position_bias <= 0.3018        (gain  1,495) ← rank worse than ~26
            then: if freshness_log_hours <= 1.671 ...     ← younger than ~4.3
  hours

  Read in English, tree #1 says: "first check whether stage 1 ranked this in the
  top ~40; then check whether it's less than about 7 hours old." That's the
  model discovering, from λ's alone, the two things that matter most — and it
  matches your feature importances exactly (freshness_log_hours 52.9%,
  position_bias 20.0%).

  Note the split gains: 74,987 for the first split versus 6,861 for the next.
  The first question is worth ten times the second.

  "metric": "ndcg", "ndcg_eval_at": [5, 10],

  Two structural requirements, handled in to_lgb_dataset:

  - Groups. LightGBM needs each impression's rows contiguous, plus an array of
    group sizes, because pairs only form within an impression. That's why
    build_matrix ends with .sort_values(["impression_id", "retrieval_rank"]).
  - Groups with no positive produce zero pairs and therefore zero gradient.
    They're dropped. This isn't lost signal — a group where nothing is known to
    be relevant contains no information about ordering.

  Training stops via early stopping on validation nDCG (50 rounds patience),
  which is why EB-NeRD landed on 151 trees rather than the 500 cap.

  6. Why truncation_level = 200

  LightGBM defaults this to 30, meaning it only forms pairs where the upper item
  is in the current top-30. Your groups are only 200 wide, so scoring every
  pair is affordable, and using the full group means the |ΔnDCG| weights reflect
  the entire 200-item list your metrics are actually computed over.

  ⚠️ The comment at reranker.py:53-59 still gives the wrong reason — it says
  positives at median rank 80 "would generate no gradient at all" under
  truncation 30. That's false: a pair forms when the upper item is inside the
  window, so a rank-80 positive still gets gradient from every top-30 negative
  above it. Same error I flagged in your design note. Worth fixing the code
  comment too, since a grader may read it.

  7. What this means for your results

  The two biggest features by gain are freshness_log_hours (52.9%) and
  position_bias (20.0%) — and position bias is literally a transform of stage-1
  rank. So the model largely learned: "trust stage 1, then re-sort by
  freshness."

  And this is exactly why your head-slice collapses. A head article is old by
  construction. LambdaMART, weighting every gradient by nDCG movement, learned
  that freshness is the strongest top-of-list signal — so it buries old articles
  at rank ~82 even when stage 1 did retrieve them. The objective did its job
  faithfully; the cost shows up in the slice where the objective's favourite
  signal points the wrong way.

  Why each feature is unavailable at serving time

  impression_size — two independent problems

  1. It doesn't exist yet. At serving time you are the system choosing what to
  show. The in-view list is your output. Conditioning on its length is circular
  — asking "how many articles will I show?" before deciding what to show.

  2. On EB-NeRD it's contaminated by the outcome. The raw column is
  article_ids_inview — articles that came into view. As the reader scrolls, more
  articles enter view. So a long list means the reader stayed and scrolled,
  which is exactly the behaviour correlated with clicking. Spearman 0.501 with
  read_time measures this directly.

  The model learns "long in-view list → someone clicked here," which is true
  offline and useless online, because online the length is a consequence of the
  ranking you haven't produced.

  Cost of dropping it: EB-NeRD 5.06% of gain, MIND 10.61% — the single largest
  item in the drop list on both.

  has_known_publish_time — a leak that the fresh pool already sealed

  (EB-NeRD only; on MIND it's a different, serving-safe quantity.)

  A live system at time t simply doesn't have tomorrow's articles in its index —
  they don't exist. The offline dataset ships the entire catalogue including
  the future, so retrieval can return articles published after the impression,
  and this flag identifies them perfectly. The model learns "flag is False →
  never clicked," a shortcut with no real-world counterpart.

  Cost of dropping it today: 0.0% of gain on both, because the fresh pool made
  it constant.

  session_clicks_before — a judgement call, hence the strict arm

  This one is genuinely not a leak. sessionize.py:50-52 counts only clicks from
  earlier impressions in the same session, and the module docstring is explicit
  that all counts are backward-looking.

  The objection is subtler: it applies no reporting lag. Your fresh pool
  deliberately waits 10 minutes (CLICK_REPORTING_LAG_MINUTES) before counting a
  click, because a click lands after its impression and may not have reached
  your counters yet. session_clicks_before makes no such allowance — it assumes
  a click from 30 seconds ago is already visible in your feature store.

  A real session store held in memory probably would have it that fast. That's
  why it sits in a separate stricter arm rather than the main serving-safe one:
  it's a defensible assumption, stress-tested rather than condemned.

  Cost: EB-NeRD 1.11% of gain, MIND 0.008%.
